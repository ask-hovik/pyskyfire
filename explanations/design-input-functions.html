Design-Input Functions¶

Most of the geometry you hand to Pyskyfire can be either a number or a function. A wall can be 1 mm thick everywhere, or it can thin out towards the nozzle exit. A rib can be 1 mm wide everywhere, or it can always take up a fifth of the local channel pitch.

A function used this way is called a design input. It declares the quantities it needs by parameter name, and Pyskyfire supplies exactly those:

sidewall_half_thickness = lambda: 0.5e-3                    # a 1 mm shared rib
sidewall_half_thickness = lambda x: 0.5e-3 + 0.5e-5 * x     # grows downstream
sidewall_half_thickness = lambda pitch_mid: 0.1 * pitch_mid # shared rib takes 20% of pitch

The parameter names are the interface. Order does not matter, and a design input that does not depend on position takes no parameters at all rather than an ignored x.

Every one of these also accepts a plain float, which is the same as a function that takes no parameters and returns that number.

sidewall_half_thickness means one complete sidewall of an isolated tube, or half of the shared rib between neighbouring chamber channels. Two 0.5 mm sidewalls form a 1 mm shared rib. Exposure fractions range from 0 to 1; exposure angles use radians. Local exchanger profiles expose their path coordinate as x; chamber-only quantities require a chamber context.

Why the tiers exist¶

The available quantities differ from one argument to the next because the geometry is built in a fixed order, and each step consumes what the previous ones produced:

contour  →  wall stack  →  channel heights  →  centrelines  →  angular widths  →  section

A design input can only be handed quantities that already exist when its own step runs. Asking for one from further down the chain would be circular: a helix angle that depended on the channel pitch could not be evaluated, because the pitch is 2π·cos(γ)/n and so depends on the helix angle.

The quantities are grouped into three tiers accordingly.

Tier A — the contour. Where you are on the chamber wall and what the wall is doing there. Known before any channel exists, so every design input can use tier A.

Tier B — layout and walls. How many channels there are and how thick the wall stack is. Known once the circuits are configured. Available everywhere except Wall.thickness, which is what defines the wall stack in the first place.

Tier C — the resolved section. The channel’s height, helix angle, pitch and radii. Known only after heights, centrelines and angular widths have been built, so it is available to sidewall_half_thickness, roughness and hot_gas_surface_area_multiplier. The two slots evaluated part of the way through see part of it: helix_angle gets h, because heights are built first, and channel_angular_width gets h and gamma. Neither can see the pitch quantities they are themselves about to determine.

A placement may be shared by several circuits, and each is evaluated against its own context. Two circuits sharing one placement but carrying different channel heights will therefore get different helix angles from the same law — which the analytical channel layout rejects, since it needs the circuits it interleaves to follow one angular evolution. If you share a placement, keep its design inputs to quantities the circuits agree on.

Examples¶

A wall that thins from 1.2 mm in the chamber to 0.8 mm at the exit, using the normalised axial coordinate rather than absolute metres:

psf.regen.Wall(
    material=material,
    thickness=lambda x_frac: 1.2e-3 - 0.4e-3 * max(x_frac, 0.0),
)

A rib of constant thickness, which is what CrossSectionSquared is built around — the rib keeps this width from the channel floor to the channel roof, so the channel itself widens with radius while the rib does not:

psf.regen.CrossSectionSquared(sidewall_half_thickness=0.5e-3)

A rib that instead always takes a fixed share of the pitch, so that the channel and the rib scale together as the chamber radius changes:

psf.regen.CrossSectionSquared(sidewall_half_thickness=lambda pitch_mid: 0.05 * pitch_mid)

This last form gives a shared rib occupying 10 % of the mid-height pitch. It removes approximately a tenth of the coolant area; the section integrates the actual curved boundary rather than approximating it as a sector minus a rectangle.

A rib sized from the raw circumference and the total channel count instead, which gives the same answer for a non-helical channel and a slightly different one for a helical one, because pitch is measured normal to the channel axis while circumference is not:

psf.regen.CrossSectionSquared(
    sidewall_half_thickness=lambda circumference, n_channels_all_circuits: (
        0.1 * circumference / n_channels_all_circuits
    ),
)

A channel height keyed to the wall it sits on:

psf.regen.CoolingCircuit(
    ...,
    channel_height=lambda t_wall: 3.0 * t_wall,
)

A constant helix angle, which needs no parameters:

psf.regen.SurfacePlacement(
    n_channel_positions=90,
    helix_angle=lambda: np.radians(30.0),
)

An area-preserving helix. A squared section holds

A ≈ h * [(pi * cos(gamma) / n) * (2 * r_base + h) - 2 * sidewall_half_thickness]

so with the channel height, rib width and channel count fixed, the coolant area is roughly constant when cos(gamma) * (2 * r_base + h) is. Anchoring that product at one station pins the helix there and opens it out as the radius grows, instead of letting the passage swell with the wall it wraps:

reference = np.cos(np.radians(20.0)) * (2.0 * (r_start + t_wall) + height)

def area_preserving_helix(r, t_wall):
    return np.arccos(
        np.clip(reference / (2.0 * (r + t_wall) + height), -1.0, 1.0)
    )

examples/advanced/sizer_sim.py uses exactly this on its nozzle circuits. Note that it closes over a fixed height rather than taking h, because the placement is shared; a single-circuit placement can take h directly. Where the channel count changes — under an overlapping return pass, say — the pitch changes with it and no helix law can hold the area across that step. The formula treats the wall as a cylinder, so on steep helices and sloping walls it can miss the computed area by several percent. Hold the coolant area constant with a helix root-finds the helix angle against the computed area instead.

A roughness that grows with the channel it roughens, which is a tier C dependency and therefore only legal in a slot that is evaluated last:

psf.regen.CoolingCircuit(..., roughness=lambda h: 0.002 * h)

Errors¶

Naming a quantity that does not exist, or one that is not available in that slot, raises DesignInputError when the owning object is constructed. The message names the offending parameter, says why it was refused, and lists what is legal there:

CrossSectionSquared.sidewall_half_thickness: 'radius' is not a design-input quantity.
Available here: area_ratio, circumference, contour, dr_dx, gamma, h, ...

Wall.thickness: 't_wall' is a tier B quantity describing the wall stack,
which is what this function defines; requesting it would be circular.
Available here: area_ratio, circumference, contour, dr_dx, ...

Design inputs are evaluated one station at a time and must return a scalar. A function is therefore free to branch on position — if x > 0.1: — without worrying about array semantics.

Two signature forms are rejected outright. *args is refused because design inputs are called with keyword arguments, so a variadic function cannot say what it wants; write out the names instead. Positional-only parameters are refused for the same reason. A **kwargs catch-all is accepted and receives every quantity available in that slot, which is occasionally useful for debugging but makes for an opaque input.

Tier C — the resolved section¶

Name

Meaning

h

Supplied channel height [m], following the cross section’s height_input convention: full height by default, optionally rounded base height.

gamma

Local helix angle of the channel, measured from the meridional direction [rad].

theta

Angular pitch of one channel measured in the plane normal to the channel axis [rad]. Already reduced by cos(gamma).

r_channel_base

Radius of the cross-section base point: the channel floor for a squared section, the inner lobe centre for a rounded one [m].

r_channel_top

Radius of the channel roof, r_channel_base + h [m].

pitch

Channel-to-channel pitch at the hot wall, r * theta [m].

pitch_base

Channel-to-channel pitch at the channel floor, r_channel_base * theta [m].

pitch_mid

Channel-to-channel pitch at mid channel height, (r_channel_base + h/2) * theta [m].

For rounded sections, height_input="base" restores the older base-height parameter. Design-input functions requesting h receive that supplied value; the algebraic r_channel_top and pitch_mid quantities above use it too. The height report defaults to the converted full internal height. See Physical cooling-channel construction for conversion and plotting examples. Keeping h in its input convention also lets helix laws use it before the angular pitch needed for full-height conversion has been resolved.

r_channel_base is resolved geometry, not r + t_wall: the section stands off the contour along its normal, so a sloping wall projects the stack outward, and a rounded section’s base point is the lobe centre its gas-area constraint solves for. Before the section has been placed — while a height or helix law is being evaluated — it falls back to the contour radius.

The three pitch quantities differ only in the radius at which the pitch is measured, and all three are measured in the plane normal to the channel axis, so they shrink with cos(gamma) on a helical channel. Which one to use depends on what the rib is being sized against: pitch_base is the material available at the rib root, while pitch_mid is the one that makes a proportional rib remove exactly that proportion of the coolant area.
