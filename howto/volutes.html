Design and solve a volute¶

A Volute collects a swirling flow around a circumference and delivers it to a round pipe. It is the shaped manifold on a pump discharge, and — through a different area law — the plain constant-area toroidal manifold sometimes used on a cooling-circuit inlet.

Three pieces are combined: a cross-section family, the inlet conditions, and an area law that decides how big the section is at each azimuth.

Define the cross-section¶

The passage cross-section is an elliptical bore fed through a parallel-sided slot, with the wall-to-bore junction rounded on both sides. The slot width is fixed by whatever feeds the volute, so the section grows only through its bore size:

import pyskyfire as psf

section = psf.ducts.EllipticalSlottedSection(
    inlet_width=0.012,    # slot width b5, fixed by the pump discharge
    fillet_radius=0.003,
    throat_length=0.002,  # straight wall before the fillet starts
)

Each fillet is tangent to its slot wall and to the bore, which is enough to place the bore centre for any bore size. The only requirement is a bore at least as wide as the slot feeding it, so section.minimum_size is inlet_width / 2.

Aspect ratio¶

aspect_ratio is the bore’s semi-axis across the slot over its semi-axis outward from the inlet plane. It defaults to one, which is a circle. Above one the bore is stretched across the slot and squashed radially, keeping the volute radially compact; below one it reaches further out than it is wide:

section = psf.ducts.EllipticalSlottedSection(
    inlet_width=0.012, fillet_radius=0.003, throat_length=0.002,
    aspect_ratio=1.6,       # oblong across the slot
    max_aspect_ratio=5.0,   # how far it may be flattened at the tongue
)

size is always the semi-axis across the slot, whatever the aspect ratio, so it plays exactly the role the bore radius does for a circle and the slot still fixes its floor at inlet_width / 2.

The bore can reach inboard of the inlet plane when it is small, which makes an intermediate rather than a fully external volute. That is valid geometry that may simply foul neighbouring hardware, so it is reported rather than rejected:

section.inboard_overhang(0.008)   # metres inboard of the inlet plane, 0.0 if clear

Lengthening throat_length pushes the bore back outboard.

Define the inlet and build the volute¶

The inlet carries the radius at which flow is collected and its two velocity components. These are design inputs: run the pump sizing first and pass its outputs in here.

inlet = psf.ducts.VoluteInlet(radius=0.060, C_m=6.0, C_u=45.0)

volute = psf.ducts.Volute(
    section, inlet, psf.ducts.ConstantAngularMomentum(), rotation="ccw"
)

Both the area law and the rotation are stated rather than defaulted. The area law decides what kind of hardware this is, and the rotation decides which way the passage winds – counterclockwise seen from +z, with the axis pointing at the viewer. It has to match the sense of the machine feeding the volute once both are placed in the assembly, because a volute wound against the incoming swirl collects the flow backwards. Like tilt, it mirrors where the passage sits and changes nothing in the loss analysis.

An inlet plane need not be radial. A pump whose discharge points a little downward, or a manifold sitting against a sloping nozzle wall, has a tilt:

volute = psf.ducts.Volute(
    section, inlet, psf.ducts.ConstantAngularMomentum(),
    rotation="ccw", tilt=math.radians(45.0),
)

Tilt is constant around the circumference and rotates the section plane rigidly, so it decides where the passage sits in space and changes nothing in the loss analysis.

ConstantAngularMomentum conserves the angular momentum being collected, which is Aungier’s method of choice. Geometry is resolved when the volute is built, so one volute can afterwards be solved at several operating points:

geometry = volute.geometry
geometry.area[-1]              # full-collection-plane area [m^2]
geometry.centroid_radius[-1]   # its centroid radius r6 [m]

The tongue¶

A shaped volute wants zero area at the tongue, and no bore that has to span the slot can shrink that far. Its shape is not stuck, though, and the aspect ratio is the way past the floor: holding the size at its minimum and flattening the bore shrinks the area roughly as one over the aspect ratio, without ever narrowing it below the slot. Volute does that automatically, up to max_aspect_ratio, so the passage tracks the area law considerably further in than a fixed circular bore could:

geometry.aspect               # resolved aspect ratio, per station
geometry.flattened            # boolean: where it was pushed past the design value
geometry.clipped              # boolean: where even the flattest section was too big
geometry.tongue_clip_angle    # azimuth up to which the law was clipped [rad]

Only where the flattest allowed section is still too big does the law get clipped, and it is clipped to that flattest section rather than to the design-aspect one. The passage right at the tongue is therefore still larger than conservation of angular momentum asks for, which is also what real hardware does, since a cutwater has finite thickness.

Setting max_aspect_ratio equal to aspect_ratio disables flattening, leaving the fixed-shape behaviour of a family with a hard area floor.

Solve for pressure loss¶

result = volute.solve(
    inlet_state=state,
    mass_flow=27.14,
    transport=transport,
    exit_diameter=0.050,
    roughness=15e-6,
)

result.SP                 # realised sizing parameter
result.loss_breakdown()   # coefficients by mechanism
result.pressure_loss      # [Pa]

Every loss coefficient is referred to the inlet velocity head, so the total pressure loss is omega_total * rho * C_5**2 / 2:

Mechanism

What it charges

meridional

The through-slot velocity head, which becomes swirl in the passage and is never recovered

tangential

Departure from conserved angular momentum between inlet and collection plane

friction

Wall shear over the mean spiral path

exit_cone

Diffusing the flow down to the round pipe’s velocity

exit_friction

Wall shear along the exit-cone duct

exit_bend

Turning the exit-cone duct

The last three need an attached ExitCone; the last two are zero without one, since there is then no duct to charge them against.

The meridional term is usually the largest, and nothing the volute does can change it — it is set by C_m at the inlet. Reducing it is a job for the pump and diffuser design upstream.

Pressure along the passage¶

result.stagnation_pressure()    # one value per azimuthal station [Pa]
result.static_pressure()
result.exit_cone_pressures(9)   # (stagnation, static) along the duct

Aungier’s analysis is lumped: it charges one coefficient per mechanism between the inlet and the collection plane and says nothing about where round the volute each is spent. Spreading them linearly with azimuth is an assumption made so the passage can be plotted and coloured at all, not a result of the model — the endpoints are what it actually pins down. The exit cone is on firmer ground, since its losses really are spent between two stations the model resolves.

Sizing parameter¶

SP is the ratio of actual passage area to the area that exactly conserves angular momentum. It is both the design knob and the parameter the tangential loss is computed from, so a designed volute realises the SP it was built at.

The default SP = 1.0 zeroes the tangential loss. That is not quite the minimum of the total loss: a slightly larger volute slows the flow at the collection plane, which buys back more in friction and exit-cone loss than the tangential term costs. Aungier recommends 1.0 to 1.2, biased upward because undersizing is penalised far more steeply than oversizing.

volute = psf.ducts.Volute(
    section, inlet, psf.ducts.ConstantAngularMomentum(SP=1.15), rotation="ccw"
)

Outlet ducts¶

A volute’s full-collection plane connects to a pipe flange whose diameter is usually fixed by a piping standard, in a direction that depends on how the machine is packaged: some outlets run straight on, some curve round until they point radially outward, and real hardware sits anywhere between. ExitCone describes that duct:

volute = psf.ducts.Volute(
    section, inlet, psf.ducts.ConstantAngularMomentum(),
    rotation="ccw",
    exit_cone=psf.ducts.ExitCone(
        final_diameter=0.050,
        first_angle="radial",       # or "tangential", or a number
        second_angle=0.0,           # elevation out of the volute's plane
        arc_length_parameter=2.0,   # duct length, in outlet diameters
    ),
)

first_angle turns within the volute’s own plane, measured from tangential – the direction the flow is already travelling as it leaves the full-collection plane. That reference is fixed at the volute outlet, since by the time the duct reaches its flange it has moved round the volute and there is no tangential direction to measure against there anymore. "radial" is different: it always means straight out from the machine axis, evaluated at wherever the flange ends up, so it is closer than a right angle away from tangential – exactly by however far the duct has travelled round. A numeric first angle beyond that bound would point the duct back inward, and is rejected.

second_angle lifts the outlet out of the volute’s own plane, on top of whatever the first angle already set. A volute with no exit cone stops at the full-collection plane, and Volute.solve() then needs an explicit exit_diameter; with one attached, the flange diameter is stated once and used for both the geometry and the loss analysis’s station 7.

The duct’s geometry reaches the loss analysis three ways, reported apart as exit_cone, exit_friction and exit_bend:

result.loss_breakdown()
# {'meridional': ..., 'tangential': ..., 'friction': ...,
#  'exit_cone': ..., 'exit_friction': ..., 'exit_bend': ...}

A widening cone is a conical diffuser, and the angle it opens out at is most of what decides its loss — so final_diameter and arc_length_parameter together set exit_cone, through the Rennels correlation. A flange narrower than the passage feeding it is a contraction instead, and takes the matching Rennels correlation for one; contractions are much the cheaper of the two, since nothing separates on the way in. The arc length also sets the wall friction is charged over, and the turn sets the bend loss.

That makes arc_length_parameter a genuine design variable with an optimum in it. Lengthening the duct opens the cone out more gradually and recovers more of the velocity head, but adds wetted wall; spreading a given turn over more duct likewise trades bend loss for friction. Too short and the cone stops diffusing and starts dumping.

A volute with no exit cone has no cone angle to resolve, and its flange is charged Aungier’s Eq. 5-110 instead — the Borda-Carnot loss of a sudden expansion, which is what an unresolved transition deserves. The Rennels correlation approaches the same value as the included angle opens toward 180 degrees, so the two agree where they should.

Visualise it¶

Volute.sweep() places each station’s section in machine coordinates, with the axis along +z and the tongue at azimuth zero. VoluteComponent wraps that into a scene. With no exit cone the sweep is capped at both ends, so the step between the two faces is the cutwater. With one attached, Volute.sweep_exit_cone() continues the same tube seamlessly out to the flange – its first ring is bit-identical to the volute’s own last one – and the cap moves there instead:

scene = psf.viz.Scene(title="Volute", color_by="velocity")
scene.add(psf.viz.VoluteComponent(volute, result), name="volute")
scene.save_html("volute.html")

Passing the result colours the passage by any per-station field: through-flow velocity, passage area, bore radius, centroid radius, or the fraction of mass flow collected so far. The exit cone is not resolved station by station, so its rings simply carry the full-collection-plane value forward.

Note that drawing a section in its own (u, v) coordinates hides the tilt, because tilt is a rotation of that frame. To see it, map into the meridional plane — examples/volute/volute_report.py does this.

Constant-area collectors¶

Swap the area law for a uniform passage — the unshaped manifold:

volute = psf.ducts.Volute(
    section, inlet, psf.ducts.ConstantArea(area=7.8e-4), rotation="ccw"
)

A collector tries to diffuse the entering flow toward rest where collection begins, so it is charged an extra half of the tangential velocity head that a shaped volute avoids. At equal collection-plane area it is substantially lossier than the shaped volute, which is the trade against its lower manufacturing cost.

In an engine network¶

VoluteBlock adapts the solver to the network, exactly as PipeBlock does:

block = psf.network.VoluteBlock(
    "discharge", "pump_out", "jacket_in", volute, transport, roughness=15e-6,
)

exit_diameter is optional here too, and defaults from an attached ExitCone the same way Volute.solve() does; pass it explicitly to override, or when the volute has no exit cone.

Scope¶

The model is Aungier’s one-dimensional inlet-to-collection-plane analysis. It does not resolve how mass flow distributes around the circumference, so it cannot yet tell you whether cooling channels far from the feed point are starved relative to near ones. It also requires incoming swirl: a manifold fed axially through a pipe, with no tangential velocity to conserve, is outside what these correlations describe.
